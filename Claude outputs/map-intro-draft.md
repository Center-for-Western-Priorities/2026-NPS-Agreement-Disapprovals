## Intro, long version (about 330 words)

On August 7, 2026, the Department of the Interior disapproved 133 financial assistance agreements the National Park Service had arranged with outside partners. The agreements would have obligated $25,947,857 at 61 parks, regional offices, and programs across all seven Park Service regions, nearly all of it for work scheduled to begin in the fiscal year that starts October 1.

The Washington Post [first reported the disapprovals](https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2026/09/11/trump-administration-blocks-25-million-funding-national-parks/) on September 11, based on internal documents. Interior spokesperson Aubrie Spady told the Post the department had disapproved agreements with groups "actively working against the best interests of the American people and the priorities of this administration," and said the decision aligns taxpayer dollars with the administration's objectives. She did not identify which agreements or organizations the department had in mind. Four Park Service staffers told the Post they received no explanation beyond a note in the records reading "Disapproved by DOI." Interior approved more than 700 other agreements this year through early August.

Financial assistance agreements are how the Park Service buys work it cannot staff itself, and Interior review is the last step before an agreement is signed and the money released. The funding behind these 133 was already secured. The work includes fire preparedness, hazard tree removal, archeological survey, invasive species control, endangered species recovery, trail crews, and seasonal positions filled by conservation corps members and retirees.

Joshua Tree National Park lost the most agreements, 10 in all, worth $1,175,678. Six of them touch fire in some way: mapping for fire response, a vegetation specialist supporting fire management decisions, and replanting in areas already burned. At Effigy Mounds National Monument in Iowa, an $80,597 agreement would have removed hazard trees from the Sny Magill unit, which holds roughly 106 Native American mounds, the largest concentration known in the United States.

One organization, the Great Basin Institute, held 72 of the 133 agreements, worth $12,229,710.

The Center for Western Priorities mapped every disapproved agreement using Park Service records. Explore it below.

## Intro, short version (about 150 words)

On August 7, 2026, the Department of the Interior disapproved 133 National Park Service agreements with outside partners, blocking $25.9 million at 61 parks, offices, and programs in all seven Park Service regions. The Washington Post [first reported the disapprovals](https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2026/09/11/trump-administration-blocks-25-million-funding-national-parks/) on September 11.

The agreements covered fire preparedness, hazard tree removal, archeological survey, endangered species recovery, trail crews, and seasonal staffing, almost all of it scheduled for the fiscal year beginning October 1. Interior told the Post it had disapproved agreements with groups working against administration priorities, and did not say which ones.

Joshua Tree National Park lost the most, 10 agreements in all. The largest single item, at $3,489,627, would have surveyed cultural resources at Lake Mead National Recreation Area on ground exposed by falling water levels.

The Center for Western Priorities mapped every disapproved agreement using Park Service records. Explore it below.

## How to explore the map

Every circle is a national park site, drawn over the outline of the park itself. Every square is a regional office, program, or monitoring network, each of which supports a group of parks rather than one. Larger markers hold more agreements. Click one to read the project description and the Park Service's own statement of what disapproval means for that site, along with the dollar figure attached to each agreement.

Filter by region in the sidebar, or search across every field. Scroll to zoom, drag to pan. The full dataset is available to download as a CSV.

## Notes for the editor

**The Post story checks out against our data.** Every example the Post used is in our
dataset with its full text, so CWP can quote the underlying records directly rather than
sourcing them to the Post. The three pull quotes above do exactly that. Four points of
comparison:

| The Post | Our dataset |
|---|---|
| "more than 130 projects ... worth at least $25 million" | 133 agreements, $25,947,857 |
| Joshua Tree "at least 10" agreements blocked | 10, worth $1,175,678 |
| Great Basin Institute "more than 70 ... worth at least $12.7 million" | 72 agreements, $12,229,710 |
| Denali sled dog kennels, three people | FAST #1821, three interns for 25 weeks, $90,001 |

**The one number that does not line up.** The Great Basin Institute told the Post its
blocked agreements were worth at least $12.7 million. Our records show $12,229,710, a
gap of about $470,000. Both figures are "at least," and the gap is smaller than the ten
unattributed IDs described below, so it is probably not a conflict. Do not print both
numbers side by side without resolving it.

**What the map adds that the story does not have.** The Post described the batch and a
handful of examples. The map has all 133, every park and office, the Park Service's own
impact language for each, and the data as a download. It also accounts for the seven
records from the August 7 batch that are no longer disapproved, which the story does not
mention.

**On phrasing.** Say "agreements worth $25.9 million," not "$25.9 million cut from the
Park Service." The figure is the federal funding each proposed action would have
obligated, not an appropriation or an annual budget line.

Say "133 agreements disapproved on August 7 and still disapproved as of September 9,"
not "133 agreements cancelled." The source batch held 140 requests. Four were approved on
Interior re-review on August 20, and three carry other statuses. A follow-up check of the
database on September 9 reconfirmed all 133 as still disapproved. See
[METHODOLOGY.md](https://github.com/Center-for-Western-Priorities/2026-NPS-Agreement-Disapprovals/blob/main/docs/METHODOLOGY.md).

**One open figure.** A second Park Service funding extract holds ten IDs worth $4,995,244
that do not appear in the disapprovals workbook at all. If they belong to this batch, the
real total is closer to $30.9 million. Worth resolving before the number goes in a
headline.

**Other material in the Post story, if you want more of it.** Peter Woodruff, the Great
Basin Institute's chief executive, said Interior never notified the institute and that he
does not know why the agreements were blocked. The Post notes the institute removed
diversity commitments from its website after 2020, and that archived copies exist.
Elaine Leslie, a former Park Service acting superintendent, asked where the $25 million
went. The Post also reports the Park Service has lost roughly a quarter of its staff since
January 2025, and that projects under $50,000 do not require Interior review. All of that
is the Post's reporting and needs attribution if used.
